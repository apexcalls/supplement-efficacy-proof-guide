How Supplement Brands Prove Their Products Work — or Don't

Every supplement brand claims its product works. The claim is easy to make and hard to prove, and the difference between a brand that has evidence and a brand that has only a slogan is usually visible in the way it presents its proof. A buyer who understands what counts as evidence can separate the two, and the place to start is the type of study the brand cites.

The first type of evidence is the randomised controlled trial. In a trial of this kind, a group of people is divided at random into two groups: one receives the supplement and the other receives a placebo, and neither group knows which it received. The results are then compared. This is the strongest form of evidence, because it controls for the placebo effect and for the natural variation between people. A brand that cites a randomised controlled trial of its own product, at the dose it sells, has strong evidence. A brand that cites a trial of a single ingredient, at a different dose, in a different population, has much weaker evidence.

The second type of evidence is the observational study. In a study of this kind, researchers observe people who already take the supplement and compare them with people who do not. The problem is that the people who choose to take a supplement may differ from those who do not in ways that affect the outcome. They may be healthier, wealthier or more health-conscious, and those differences can explain the result rather than the supplement. An observational study can suggest that a supplement might work, but it cannot prove that it does. A brand that relies on observational studies alone is presenting a suggestion as a fact.

The third type of evidence is the testimonial. A testimonial is a story from a customer who says the product worked for them. Testimonials are persuasive because they are personal, but they are the weakest form of evidence. The customer may have improved for another reason, may have changed other habits at the same time, or may have been selected by the brand because their story was positive. A brand that presents testimonials as its main evidence has no evidence at all. A brand that presents testimonials alongside a controlled trial is using them as illustration, not as proof.

The fourth thing to check is whether the study tested the product itself or only an ingredient in it. Many supplements contain several ingredients, and the brand may cite a study of one ingredient while implying that the study supports the whole product. A study of vitamin D does not prove that a multivitamin containing vitamin D works. A study of a botanical extract at a dose of 500 milligrams does not prove that a product containing 50 milligrams of that extract works. The buyer should check whether the study matches the product: the same ingredients, the same dose and the same population. If any of those differ, the study is not evidence for the product on the shelf.

The practical response is to ask the brand for the study. A brand with evidence will provide a link to the published paper, with the dose, the population and the results clearly stated. A brand without evidence will offer a testimonial, a summary written by its own marketing team, or a study of a different product. The buyer who reads the study, rather than the brand's summary of it, will know whether the evidence is real. The buyer who accepts the summary is trusting the brand to describe its own evidence honestly.
